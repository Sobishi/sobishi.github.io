Inventory Accuracy Is Not a Cycle Count Problem

Why improving inventory accuracy requires more than counting stock.

Inventory accuracy is often treated as a counting problem. If the physical stock does not match the system, the immediate response is usually to count again.

But what happens when the same discrepancies keep appearing?

At that point, counting is no longer solving the problem. It is measuring the symptom.

The problem I encountered

In one of the operations I managed, inventory accuracy had fallen to approximately 45%. It had become a situation where other countries and leadership across the region no longer trusted the numbers that came from us in order to plan, meetings had gradually turned to arguments about who's data was right. which translated to wasted time, rather than having meaningful discussions and making decisions based on the inventory data, it became really embarrasing.

The operation involved multiple warehouse and field stocking locations, more than 1,000 active SKUs and inventory valued at around $2 million.

At that scale, inventory accuracy is not simply a warehouse metric. It affects service availability, purchasing decisions,logistics, working capital, planning, order management, customer experience and even integrity of the workers.

Counting more was not the answer

The obvious response would have been to increase the frequency of physical counts.

But a count only tells you that reality and the system disagree. It does not necessarily tell you why they disagree.

I started looking at the problem differently.

Instead of asking:

"Why is the stock wrong?"

I started asking:

"Where does physical reality diverge from the system?"

Following the transaction

I looked at the movement of inventory through the operation and examined how physical activities were translated into system transactions.

Receiving, transfers, issues, returns, adjustments and other movements each created opportunities for the physical inventory and the system record to become disconnected.

This changed the nature of the problem.

Instead of treating every discrepancy as an isolated counting error, I began looking for patterns in how the discrepancies were being created.

The systems view

An inventory record is ultimately the result of a chain of operational events.

A simplified version looks something like this:

Physical movement ↓ Operational process ↓ System transaction ↓ Inventory record

If any part of that chain is poorly controlled, the inventory record can become unreliable.

This is why inventory accuracy is partly a systems problem.

What changed

I focused on standardising warehouse execution, strengthening transaction discipline and introducing structured cycle-counting practices.

The objective was not simply to correct individual discrepancies. It was to reduce the number of discrepancies being created in the first place.

45% → 98% Inventory accuracy improvement

The lesson I took from it

One of the most useful lessons was that operational problems often have layers.

The visible problem might be inaccurate inventory. Underneath that could be a process problem. Underneath the process problem could be a system design or transaction problem.

If you only fix the visible symptom, the problem is likely to return.

This is one reason I became increasingly interested in enterprise systems and Dynamics 365 Supply Chain Management.

Why this matters for the future of supply chain

As supply chains become increasingly digitised, the quality of operational decisions will depend increasingly on the quality of the information flowing through the system.

Analytics can identify patterns.

Automation can reduce repetitive work.

AI can help investigate and interpret information.

But none of these technologies can compensate indefinitely for poorly designed operational processes.

The opportunity is therefore not simply to automate supply chains.

It is to build better operational systems in which physical execution, business processes, enterprise systems, data and intelligent decision support reinforce one another.
